\section*{I. Vectors}

\subsection*{I.1--2. Coordinates and displacements}

A vector in \(\R^n\) is an ordered list \(x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) of real
numbers. Two vectors are equal when their corresponding coordinates are
equal. For \(x,y\in\R^n\) and \(c\in\R\), define
\[
x+y=(x_1+y_1,\ldots,x_n+y_n),\qquad
cx=(cx_1,\ldots,cx_n).
\]
The zero vector is \(0=(0,\ldots,0)\), and \(x-y=x+(-1)y\).
These operations satisfy
\begin{align*}
x+y&=y+x,&(x+y)+z&=x+(y+z),\\
c(x+y)&=cx+cy,&(c+d)x&=cx+dx,\\
c(dx)&=(cd)x,&1x&=x,\\
x+0&=x,&x+(-x)&=0.
\end{align*}
Each identity follows by applying the corresponding real-number identity
to every coordinate. A \textbf{linear combination} of vectors
\(v_1,\ldots,v_k\) is a vector \(c_1v_1+\cdots+c_kv_k\), with real coefficients.

The same coordinates can specify a point. The displacement from a point
\(p\) to a point \(q\) is \(q-p\); translating both points by \(a\) leaves it
unchanged:
\[
(q+a)-(p+a)=q-p.
\]

\subsection*{I.3. Scalar product}

The \textbf{scalar product}, or \textbf{dot product}, of \(x,y\in\R^n\) is
\[
x\cdot y=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i.
\]
For example, \((1,3,-2)\cdot(-1,4,-3)=-1+12+6=17\).
For \(x,y,z\in\R^n\) and \(c,d\in\R\),
\begin{align*}
x\cdot y&=y\cdot x,\\
x\cdot(cy+dz)&=c(x\cdot y)+d(x\cdot z),\\
x\cdot x&\geqslant0,\qquad x\cdot x=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x=0.
\end{align*}
Symmetry follows from \(x_i y_i=y_i x_i\). To verify the second identity,
expand the coordinates before collecting the two sums:
\[
x\cdot(cy+dz)=\sum_i x_i(cy_i+dz_i)
=c\sum_i x_iy_i+d\sum_i x_iz_i
=c(x\cdot y)+d(x\cdot z).
\]
Symmetry gives the same rule in the first argument. Finally,
\(x\cdot x=\sum_i x_i^2\). Every summand is nonnegative; if \(x\ne0\),
some \(x_j\ne0\), so the sum is at least \(x_j^2>0\).

Vectors \(x,y\) are \textbf{orthogonal}, written \(x\perp y\), if
\(x\cdot y=0\). The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector. Let \(e_i\)
have coordinate 1 in position \(i\) and 0 elsewhere. Then
\[
x=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i e_i,\qquad x\cdot e_i=x_i,\qquad
e_i\cdot e_j=\begin{cases}1,&i=j,\\0,&i\ne j.\end{cases}
\]

\subsection*{I.4. Norm, projection, and angles}

The \textbf{norm} of \(x\) and the \textbf{distance} between \(x\) and \(y\) are
\[
\norm{x}=\sqrt{x\cdot x},\qquad d(x,y)=\norm{x-y}.
\]
In the plane, the classical Pythagorean theorem identifies
\(\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}\) with the length of the vector. In space, applying
it first in the coordinate plane and then in the perpendicular direction
gives \(\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2}\). The norm extends this length formula
to \(\R^n\).
Thus \(\norm{x}\geqslant0\), with equality exactly when \(x=0\), and
\[
\norm{cx}=\sqrt{c^2(x\cdot x)}=|c|\norm{x}.
\]
A vector has \textbf{unit norm} if its norm is 1. For \(x\ne0\),
\[
\left\lVert\frac{x}{\norm{x}}\right\rVert
=\frac{1}{\norm{x}}\norm{x}=1.
\]
Two nonzero vectors have the \textbf{same direction} when one is a
positive multiple of the other. Thus \(x/\norm{x}\) is the unit vector
in the direction of \(x\), and
\(x=\norm{x}\bigl(x/\norm{x}\bigr)\).

For a center \(a\) and radius \(r>0\), the sets
\[
\{x:\norm{x-a}<r\},\qquad
\{x:\norm{x-a}\leqslant r\},\qquad
\{x:\norm{x-a}=r\}
\]
are the \textbf{open ball}, \textbf{closed ball}, and \textbf{sphere}.
Every point of the sphere is \(a+ru\) for a unit vector \(u\): one
direction follows from \(\norm{ru}=r\), and the other from
\(u=(x-a)/r\). In \(\R^2\) the sphere is a circle.

\begin{proposition}[Pythagoras]\label{prop:01:pythagoras}
For \(x,y\in\R^n\),
\[
\norm{x+y}^2=\norm{x}^2+2x\cdot y+\norm{y}^2.
\]
Consequently, \(x\perp y\) if and only if
\(\norm{x+y}^2=\norm{x}^2+\norm{y}^2\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Using the scalar-product identities,
\begin{align*}
\norm{x+y}^2&=(x+y)\cdot(x+y)\\
&=x\cdot x+x\cdot y+y\cdot x+y\cdot y\\
&=\norm{x}^2+2x\cdot y+\norm{y}^2.
\end{align*}
Thus the norm identity without the cross term holds exactly when
\(2x\cdot y=0\), which is the definition of orthogonality.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Projection onto a line]\label{prop:01:projection}
Let \(b\ne0\). For each \(x\in\R^n\), there is a unique real number \(c\)
such that \(x-cb\perp b\), namely
\[
c=\frac{x\cdot b}{b\cdot b}.
\]
The vector \(p=cb\) is the \textbf{orthogonal projection} of \(x\) onto the
line of multiples of \(b\). Writing \(r=x-p\),
\[
x=p+r,\qquad p\perp r,\qquad
\norm{x}^2=\norm{p}^2+\norm{r}^2.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We seek a multiple \(cb\) whose difference from \(x\) is perpendicular
to \(b\). The condition is
\[
0=(x-cb)\cdot b=x\cdot b-c(b\cdot b).
\]
Since \(b\cdot b>0\), this equation forces the displayed value of \(c\).
Conversely, that value gives
\[
\left(x-\frac{x\cdot b}{b\cdot b}b\right)\cdot b
=x\cdot b-\frac{x\cdot b}{b\cdot b}(b\cdot b)=0.
\]
Hence the required multiple exists and is unique. With \(p=cb\) and
\(r=x-cb\), we have \(p\cdot r=c(b\cdot r)=0\), so Pythagoras applies
to \(x=p+r\).
\end{proof}

The coefficient \(c\) is a scalar; the projection \(cb\) is a vector. When
\(b\) has unit norm, \(c=x\cdot b\).

\begin{example}\label{example:01:projection}

\[

\]
Thus \(r\cdot b=0\), and Pythagoras gives
\(\norm{x}^2=10=8+2=\norm{p}^2+\norm{r}^2\).

\paragraph{The geometric angle.}
For two nonzero vectors in the plane or in space, place their tails at
the same point. Their geometric angle \(\theta\) lies in \([0,\pi]\).
The following proof uses ordinary Euclidean lengths and right-triangle
trigonometry.

\begin{proposition}[Cosine law]\label{prop:01:cosine-law}
If two sides of a triangle have lengths \(p,q>0\), their included angle
is \(\theta\), and the opposite side has length \(c\), then
\[
c^2=p^2+q^2-2pq\cos\theta.
\]
The formula also holds for a degenerate triangle with \(\theta=0\) or
\(\theta=\pi\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let the vertices be \(O,P,Q\), with \(|OP|=p\), \(|OQ|=q\), and
\(\angle POQ=\theta\). For \(0<\theta<\pi\), drop a perpendicular
from \(Q\) to the line through \(O,P\), meeting it at \(H\).
Let \(t\) be the signed distance from \(O\) to \(H\), positive in the
direction of \(P\), and put \(h=|HQ|\).

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.9, every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \coordinate (O) at (0,0);
  \coordinate (P) at (3,0);
  \coordinate (Q) at (-1.2,1.9);
  \coordinate (H) at (-1.2,0);
  \draw[thick] (O)--node[below]{\(p\)}(P)
      --node[above right]{\(c\)}(Q)--node[right]{\(q\)}cycle;
  \draw[dashed] (O)--(H)--node[left]{\(h\)}(Q);
  \draw (-1.2,.16)--(-1.04,.16)--(-1.04,0);
  \draw (.52,0) arc[start angle=0,end angle=122.3,radius=.52];
  \node at (.34,.64){\(\theta\)};
  \node[below] at (O){\(O\)};
  \node[below] at (P){\(P\)};
  \node[above] at (Q){\(Q\)};
  \node[below] at (H){\(H\)};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

For an acute angle, \(t=q\cos\theta\). For a right angle, both sides
are zero. For an obtuse angle, \(H\) lies on the opposite ray and
\(t=-q\cos(\pi-\theta)=q\cos\theta\). The classical geometric
Pythagorean theorem gives
\(t^2+h^2=q^2\) and
\[
c^2=(p-t)^2+h^2
    =p^2-2pt+(t^2+h^2)
    =p^2+q^2-2pq\cos\theta.
\]
The squared distance \((p-t)^2\) also covers a foot beyond \(P\).
If \(\theta=0\), then \(c=|p-q|\); if \(\theta=\pi\), then \(c=p+q\).
Substitution proves the two degenerate cases.
\end{proof}

Apply the cosine law to the triangle with vertices \(0,a,b\), for
nonzero \(a,b\in\R^2\) or \(\R^3\). Euclidean distance agrees with the
coordinate norm by Pythagoras, so
\[
\norm{a-b}^2=\norm{a}^2+\norm{b}^2
             -2\norm{a}\norm{b}\cos\theta.
\]
On the other hand, expansion of the coordinate dot product gives
\[
\norm{a-b}^2=(a-b)\cdot(a-b)
             =\norm{a}^2+\norm{b}^2-2a\cdot b.
\]
Comparing the two expressions yields the geometric formula
\[
\boxed{a\cdot b=\norm{a}\norm{b}\cos\theta.}
\]
Thus the coordinate definition of the dot product agrees with the
geometric definition \(\norm{a}\norm{b}\cos\theta\). For nonzero vectors,
\(a\cdot b=0\) is equivalent to \(\theta=\pi/2\).

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
The coordinate bound \(|x_i|\leqslant\norm{x}\) follows from
\(x_i^2\leqslant\sum_jx_j^2\). Projection gives the same bound along
any unit direction \(e\). Put \(c=x\cdot e\). Then
\[
x=(x-ce)+ce,\qquad
\norm{x}^2=\norm{x-ce}^2+c^2\geqslant c^2,
\]
so \(|x\cdot e|\leqslant\norm{x}\). The next theorem allows an
arbitrary second vector.

\begin{theorem}[Schwarz inequality]\label{thm:01:schwarz}
For all \(x,y\in\R^n\),
\[
|x\cdot y|\leqslant\norm{x}\norm{y}.
\]
If \(y\ne0\), equality holds exactly when \(x\) is a scalar multiple of
\(y\). If \(y=0\), equality always holds.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The case \(y=0\) gives \(0=0\). For \(y\ne0\), let
\(c=(x\cdot y)/\norm{y}^2\) and \(r=x-cy\). By
Proposition~\ref{prop:01:projection},
\[
\norm{x}^2=\norm{r}^2+c^2\norm{y}^2
=\norm{r}^2+\frac{(x\cdot y)^2}{\norm{y}^2}
\geqslant\frac{(x\cdot y)^2}{\norm{y}^2}.
\]
Multiply by \(\norm{y}^2\) and take nonnegative square roots.
Equality holds exactly when \(\norm{r}=0\), equivalently \(x=cy\).
\end{proof}

For nonzero \(a,b\in\R^n\), Schwarz gives
\[
-1\leqslant\frac{a\cdot b}{\norm{a}\norm{b}}\leqslant1.
\]
We therefore \textbf{define the angle} between them to be the unique
\(\theta\in[0,\pi]\) such that
\[
\cos\theta=\frac{a\cdot b}{\norm{a}\norm{b}},
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
\theta=\arccos\frac{a\cdot b}{\norm{a}\norm{b}}.
\]
This extends the geometric angle in \(\R^2\) and \(\R^3\). An angle with
the zero vector is undefined. For nonzero vectors, a positive, zero, or
negative dot product means respectively \(\theta<\pi/2\),
\(\theta=\pi/2\), or \(\theta>\pi/2\).

\begin{proposition}[Triangle inequality]\label{prop:01:triangle}
For \(x,y\in\R^n\), \(\norm{x+y}\leqslant\norm{x}+\norm{y}\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expanding and applying Schwarz gives
\[
\norm{x+y}^2
=\norm{x}^2+2x\cdot y+\norm{y}^2
\leqslant\norm{x}^2+2\norm{x}\norm{y}+\norm{y}^2
=(\norm{x}+\norm{y})^2.
\]
Both sides of the claimed inequality are nonnegative, so taking square
roots preserves the inequality.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{I.5. Parametric lines}

The line through \(a\in\R^n\) with direction \(d\ne0\) is
\[
\ell=\{a+td:t\in\R\}.
\]
For distinct points \(a,q\), take \(d=q-a\). Restricting \(0\leqslant t\leqslant1\)
gives the segment joining the two points:
\[
a+t(q-a)=(1-t)a+tq,\qquad
a+0(q-a)=a,\quad a+1(q-a)=q.
\]
The distance traveled from \(a\) is \(\norm{t(q-a)}=t\norm{q-a}\),
so the parameter is the fraction of the segment traversed.
Differences of points on
\(\ell\) are multiples of \(d\):
\[
(a+td)-(a+sd)=(t-s)d.
\]
Replacing \(d\) by \(cd\), for \(c\ne0\), gives the same line because
\(ct\) still ranges over all real numbers. If \(c<0\), increasing the
new parameter moves in the opposite direction.

For \(a=(1,-3,4)\), \(q=(5,1,-2)\), the point one third of the way
from \(a\) to \(q\) is
\[
a+\tfrac13(q-a)=(1,-3,4)+\tfrac13(4,4,-6)
=(7/3,-5/3,2).
\]

\begin{example}\label{example:01:line}
Take \(a=(1,0,1)\) and \(d=(1,1,0)\). Then
\[
x=a+td=(1+t,t,1)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}x_1-x_2=1,\\x_3=1.\end{cases}
\]
The parameterization implies both equations. Conversely, if the equations
hold, setting \(t=x_2\) recovers the parameterization.
\end{example}

\subsection*{I.6. Planes}

A plane through \(a\in\R^3\), with nonzero \textbf{normal vector} \(n\),
has equation
\[
n\cdot(x-a)=0,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad n\cdot x=n\cdot a.
\]
If \(p,q\) lie in the plane, then \(n\cdot(q-p)=0\): the normal is
perpendicular to displacements within the plane. A position vector \(p\)
need not be perpendicular to \(n\).
For \(n=(n_1,n_2,n_3)\), the equation reads
\[
n_1x_1+n_2x_2+n_3x_3=n\cdot a.
\]
Thus the coefficients of a plane equation give its normal. Multiplying
the equation by any nonzero scalar changes neither its solutions nor
the line of normal vectors; a negative scalar reverses the normal.
In \(\R^2\), the equation \(n\cdot(x-a)=0\)
defines the line through \(a\) perpendicular to \(n\).

More generally, for \(n\in\R^k\setminus\{0\}\), the equation
\(n\cdot x=\alpha\) defines a \textbf{hyperplane}. It contains the origin
exactly when \(\alpha=0\). If \(a\) is one solution, then
\[
\{x:n\cdot x=\alpha\}
=\{a+v:n\cdot v=0\}.
\]
This follows by substituting \(x=a+v\) and subtracting \(n\cdot a=\alpha\).

\begin{example}\label{example:01:plane}
The plane with normal \(n=(1,-1,0)\) through \(a=(1,0,1)\) is
\[
x_1-x_2=1.
\]
It contains the line in Example~\ref{example:01:line}, since
\(n\cdot d=0\). The point \((1,0,0)\) is in the plane but not on that line:
the line also requires \(x_3=1\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A plane through three points]\label{example:01:three-points}
Find a plane through \(p_1=(1,2,-1)\), \(p_2=(-1,1,4)\), and
\(p_3=(1,3,-2)\). A normal \(n=(a,b,c)\) must satisfy
\[
n\cdot(p_2-p_1)=0,\qquad n\cdot(p_3-p_1)=0.
\]
Since \(p_2-p_1=(-2,-1,5)\) and \(p_3-p_1=(0,1,-1)\), these are
\[
-2a-b+5c=0,\qquad b-c=0.
\]
Taking \(b=c=1\) gives \(a=2\). Hence \(n=(2,1,1)\) is a nonzero
normal, and the plane is
\[
2x_1+x_2+x_3=n\cdot p_1=3.
\]
Substituting each of the three points gives \(3\).
\end{example}

Two planes are parallel when their normals are parallel, and
perpendicular when their normals have dot product zero. Choosing
normals \(n,m\) determines an angle by
\(\cos\theta=(n\cdot m)/(\norm n\norm m)\). Reversing one normal
replaces \(\theta\) by \(\pi-\theta\). The smaller, unoriented angle
\(\phi\in[0,\pi/2]\) therefore satisfies
\[
\cos\phi=\frac{|n\cdot m|}{\norm n\norm m}.
\]
For \(2x_1-x_2+x_3=0\) and \(x_1+2x_2-x_3=1\), the displayed
normals are \((2,-1,1)\), \((1,2,-1)\). Their dot product is \(-1\)
and both have norm \(\sqrt6\); thus \(\cos\theta=-1/6\) and
\(\cos\phi=1/6\).

\begin{proposition}[Distance from a point to a plane]\label{prop:01:plane-distance}
Let \(H=\{x:n\cdot(x-a)=0\}\), where \(n\ne0\). For a point \(q\),
the perpendicular foot on \(H\) and the distance to \(H\) are
\[
q'=q-\frac{(q-a)\cdot n}{\norm n^2}n,\qquad
\operatorname{dist}(q,H)=\frac{|(q-a)\cdot n|}{\norm n}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Set \(e=n/\norm n\), a unit normal. The component of \(q-a\) along
\(e\) is \(c=(q-a)\cdot e\). Subtracting this normal component gives
the candidate foot \(q'=q-ce\).
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.8,>=Stealth,every node/.style={font=\small}]
\draw (-.5,0)--(4,0) node[right]{\(H\), in cross-section};
\draw[->,thick] (0,0)--(3,2) node[midway,above left]{\(q-a\)};
\draw[->,thick] (3,0)--(3,2) node[midway,right]{\(ce\)};
\draw[->] (0,0)--(3,0) node[midway,below]{\(q'-a\)};
\draw (2.8,0)--(2.8,.2)--(3,.2);
\node[below left] at (0,0){\(a\)};
\node[below right] at (3,0){\(q'\)};
\node[above] at (3,2){\(q\)};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Indeed,
\[
(q'-a)\cdot e=(q-a)\cdot e-c(e\cdot e)=c-c=0,
\]
so \(q'\in H\), and \(q-q'=ce\) is perpendicular to the plane.
For any \(x\in H\), the displacement \(q'-x\) is perpendicular to
\(e\). Therefore
\[
q-x=ce+(q'-x),\qquad
\norm{q-x}^2=c^2+\norm{q'-x}^2\geqslant c^2.
\]
Equality holds exactly at \(x=q'\). The shortest distance is consequently
\[
\norm{ce}=|c|=\frac{|(q-a)\cdot n|}{\norm n}.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{example:01:plane-distance}
For \(q=(1,3,5)\) and the plane \(-x_1+x_2-x_3=-5\), take
\(a=(-1,1,7)\), \(n=(-1,1,-1)\). Then
\[
q-a=(2,2,-2),\qquad(q-a)\cdot n=2,\qquad\norm n=\sqrt3.
\]
The distance is \(2/\sqrt3\), and the foot is
\[
q'=q-\tfrac23n=(5/3,7/3,17/3).
\]
Its coordinates satisfy \(-5/3+7/3-17/3=-5\), as required.
\end{example}

\section*{II. Matrices and linear equations}

\subsection*{II.1. Matrices}

An \(m\times n\) matrix \(A=(a_{ij})\) has \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns;
\(a_{ij}\) is the entry in row \(i\), column \(j\). Two matrices are equal
when they have the same size and equal corresponding entries.
For matrices of the same size,
\[
(A+B)_{ij}=a_{ij}+b_{ij},\qquad (cA)_{ij}=ca_{ij}.
\]
The zero matrix has every entry zero. A matrix is \textbf{square} when
\(m=n\).
Addition and scalar multiplication satisfy the same eight rules as for
vectors, because each rule holds at every entry. For example,
\[
\bigl(c(A+B)\bigr)_{ij}=c(a_{ij}+b_{ij})
=ca_{ij}+cb_{ij}=(cA+cB)_{ij}.
\]

The \textbf{transpose} \(A^\top\) is the \(n\times m\) matrix with
\((A^\top)_{ji}=a_{ij}\). Thus
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A^\top=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
A square matrix is \textbf{symmetric} if \(A^\top=A\). Entrywise definitions
give
\[
(A^\top)^\top=A,\qquad (A+B)^\top=A^\top+B^\top,\qquad(cA)^\top=cA^\top.
\]

In matrix products, vectors are columns:
\[
x=\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\\vdots\\x_n\end{pmatrix},\qquad
x^\top=(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
\]
The tuples used in Chapter I specify these same coordinates; a row vector
in a matrix product is written explicitly as a transpose.

\subsection*{II.2. Multiplication of matrices}

For \(A\in\R^{m\times n}\) and \(B\in\R^{n\times p}\), define
\[
AB\in\R^{m\times p},\qquad
(AB)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}b_{kj}.
\]
The shared size \(n\) is required: an entry of \(AB\) is the dot product
of a row of \(A\) and a column of \(B\).

\begin{example}\label{example:01:product}
For
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&-1\\0&2\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
AB=\begin{pmatrix}
1(2)+2(1)-1(0)&1(0)+2(-1)-1(2)\\
0(2)+1(1)+3(0)&0(0)+1(-1)+3(2)
\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}4&-4\\1&5\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\end{example}

If \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) are the columns of \(A\), the entry formula also gives
\[
Ax=x_1a_1+\cdots+x_na_n,\qquad
AB=\begin{pmatrix}Ab_1&\cdots&Ab_p\end{pmatrix},
\]
where \(b_j\) is column \(j\) of \(B\). For
Example~\ref{example:01:product}, the first column of \(AB\) is
\[
2a_1+a_2=2\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
+\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}4\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
In particular,
\[
Ax=b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}x_j=b_i\quad(i=1,\ldots,m).
\]
For the matrix \(A\) above, \(Ax=(4,1)^\top\) means
\(x_1+2x_2-x_3=4\) and \(x_2+3x_3=1\).

For column vectors \(x,y\in\R^n\),
\[
x^\top y=x\cdot y\quad\text{is a scalar},\qquad
xy^\top=(x_i y_j)_{ij}\quad\text{is an }n\times n\text{ matrix}.
\]

\paragraph{Product laws.}
Whenever the products and sums have compatible sizes,
\begin{gather*}
A(B+C)=AB+AC,\qquad (A+B)C=AC+BC,\\
(cA)B=A(cB)=c(AB),\qquad (AB)C=A(BC).
\end{gather*}
Distributivity and scalar compatibility follow by distributing each entry's
sum. More explicitly, let \(r_i\) be row \(i\) of \(A\), and let
\(b_j,c_j\) be column \(j\) of \(B,C\). Then
\[
\bigl(A(B+C)\bigr)_{ij}
=r_i\cdot(b_j+c_j)=r_i\cdot b_j+r_i\cdot c_j
=(AB+AC)_{ij}.
\]
The equality \(r_i\cdot(cb_j)=c(r_i\cdot b_j)\) proves scalar
compatibility. Applying the same calculation to a sum of rows proves
\((A+B)C=AC+BC\).

\textit{Associativity proof (reading).} If \(A,B,C\) have sizes \(m\times n\), \(n\times p\),
\(p\times q\), respectively, both sides have entry
\begin{align*}
\bigl((AB)C\bigr)_{ij}
&=\sum_{k=1}^p(AB)_{ik}c_{kj}
=\sum_{k=1}^p\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^n a_{i\ell}b_{\ell k}\right)c_{kj}\\
&=\sum_{\ell=1}^n a_{i\ell}
  \left(\sum_{k=1}^p b_{\ell k}c_{kj}\right)
=\sum_{\ell=1}^n a_{i\ell}(BC)_{\ell j}
=\bigl(A(BC)\bigr)_{ij}.
\end{align*}
The interchange is valid because both sums are finite. Associativity
permits writing \(ABC\).

The \textbf{identity matrix} \(I_n\) has diagonal entries 1 and all other
entries 0. For \(A\in\R^{m\times n}\), the entry formula gives
\[
I_mA=A=AI_n.
\]
Indeed, column \(j\) of \(AI_n\) is \(Ae_j\), the \(j\)th column
of \(A\), while row \(i\) of \(I_mA\) is row \(i\) of \(A\).
The matrix \(cI_n\) is called a \textbf{scalar matrix}.
For square \(A\), define \(A^0=I_n\), \(A^1=A\), and
\(A^{k+1}=A^kA\). Associativity gives
\[
A^rA^s=A^{r+s}\qquad(r,s\geqslant0).
\]

\begin{example}[Population transitions]\label{example:01:markov}
Let \(x_k,y_k,z_k\) be the populations of Los Angeles, Chicago, and
Boston in year \(k\). Each year the fractions moving are
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
\text{from}\backslash\text{to}&\text{Los Angeles}&\text{Chicago}&\text{Boston}\\\hline
\text{Los Angeles}&\text{remain}&1/7&1/4\\
\text{Chicago}&1/5&\text{remain}&1/3\\
\text{Boston}&1/6&1/8&\text{remain}
\end{array}
\]
The fractions remaining are \(1-1/7-1/4=17/28\),
\(1-1/5-1/3=7/15\), and \(1-1/6-1/8=17/24\). Thus
\begin{align*}
x_{k+1}&=\tfrac{17}{28}x_k+\tfrac15y_k+\tfrac16z_k,\\
y_{k+1}&=\tfrac17x_k+\tfrac7{15}y_k+\tfrac18z_k,\\
z_{k+1}&=\tfrac14x_k+\tfrac13y_k+\tfrac{17}{24}z_k.
\end{align*}
Writing populations as a column gives
\[
X_{k+1}=AX_k,\qquad
A=\begin{pmatrix}
17/28&1/5&1/6\\1/7&7/15&1/8\\1/4&1/3&17/24
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
X_k=\begin{pmatrix}x_k\\y_k\\z_k\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Column \(j\) records where the population of city \(j\) goes.
Each column sums to \(1\), so adding the three recurrence equations
gives
\[
x_{k+1}+y_{k+1}+z_{k+1}=x_k+y_k+z_k.
\]
A square matrix with nonnegative entries and column sums \(1\) is a
\textbf{Markov matrix} under this column-vector convention. Repeated
application gives
\[
X_2=AX_1,\quad X_3=A(AX_1)=A^2X_1,\quad
X_{k+1}=A^kX_1.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Transpose of a product]\label{prop:01:transpose}
If \(AB\) is defined, then \((AB)^\top=B^\top A^\top\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For \(A\in\R^{m\times n}\), \(B\in\R^{n\times p}\), both matrices on the
right and left have size \(p\times m\), and
\[
\bigl((AB)^\top\bigr)_{ij}
=(AB)_{ji}=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{jk}b_{ki}
=\sum_{k=1}^n (B^\top)_{ik}(A^\top)_{kj}
=(B^\top A^\top)_{ij}.
\]
\end{proof}
Consequently \(Ax=b\) can also be written
\(x^\top A^\top=b^\top\), with the order reversed.

\begin{example}\label{example:01:noncommute}
Let
\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
N=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Then \(PN=N\), whereas \(NP=0\). Thus matrix multiplication need not
commute, and a product of two nonzero matrices may be zero.
Also \(P^2=PI_2=P\), although \(P\ne I_2\); cancelling a nonzero matrix
from an equation is not valid in general.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Inverses.}
An \textbf{inverse} of a square matrix \(A\in\R^{n\times n}\) is an
\(n\times n\) matrix \(B\) satisfying both \(AB=I_n\) and \(BA=I_n\).
A matrix with an inverse is \textbf{invertible}. The inverse is unique:
if \(B\) and \(C\) are inverses, then
\[
B=BI_n=B(AC)=(BA)C=I_nC=C.
\]
We denote it by \(A^{-1}\).

For example,
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad AB=BA=I_2,
\]
so \(B=A^{-1}\). In contrast, the matrix \(P\) in
Example~\ref{example:01:noncommute} has no inverse: every product \(PC\)
has zero second row and therefore cannot equal \(I_2\).

If \(A\) is invertible and \(AX=AY\), multiplication on the left by
\(A^{-1}\) gives \(X=Y\). This cancellation uses invertibility, not merely
\(A\ne0\).

Transposing the two inverse identities gives
\[
(A^{-1})^\top A^\top=(AA^{-1})^\top=I_n,\qquad
A^\top(A^{-1})^\top=(A^{-1}A)^\top=I_n.
\]
Thus \(A^\top\) is invertible and
\((A^\top)^{-1}=(A^{-1})^\top\).

\begin{example}[Rotations]\label{example:01:rotation}
For \(\theta\in\R\), put
\[
R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
\]
A unit vector in the plane has the form
\(u=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)^\top\). Multiplication gives
\[
R(\theta)u
=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta\cos\varphi-\sin\theta\sin\varphi\\
\sin\theta\cos\varphi+\cos\theta\sin\varphi
\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta+\varphi)\\\sin(\theta+\varphi)\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The last equality is the pair of trigonometric addition formulas.
For any nonzero \(x=ru\), where \(r=\norm{x}\),
\(R(\theta)x=rR(\theta)u\): multiplication preserves length and turns
the direction through \(\theta\). It fixes the zero vector.
For example, rotating \((2,5)^\top\) through \(\pi/3\) gives
\[
\begin{pmatrix}1/2&-\sqrt3/2\\\sqrt3/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}2\\5\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1-5\sqrt3/2\\\sqrt3+5/2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
If coordinates are written as a row, the same rotation is
\(x^\top R(\theta)^\top\), by the transpose-product rule.
\end{example}

\subsection*{II.3. Homogeneous linear equations and elimination}

For \(A=(a_{ij})\in\R^{m\times n}\), \(b\in\R^m\), and an unknown
column \(x\in\R^n\), the system
\[
\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}x_j=b_i\quad(1\leqslant i\leqslant m)
\qquad\text{is written}\qquad Ax=b.
\]
It has \(m\) equations and \(n\) unknowns. A \textbf{solution} is a column
whose entries satisfy every equation. The system is \textbf{consistent}
if it has a solution, and \textbf{homogeneous} if \(b=0\).
The homogeneous system always has the \textbf{trivial solution} \(x=0\);
a \textbf{nontrivial solution} has at least one nonzero coordinate.
If the rows of \(A\) are \(r_1^\top,\ldots,r_m^\top\), then
\[
Ax=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
r_i\cdot x=0\ \text{for every }i.
\]

\begin{example}[Eliminate one variable, then recover it]\label{example:01:two-equations}
Consider
\[
2x+3y-z=0,\qquad x+y+z=0.
\]
Subtracting twice the second equation from the first gives \(y-3z=0\).
Set \(z=t\). Then \(y=3t\), and the second original equation gives
\(x=-y-z=-4t\). The first original equation is also satisfied, since
\[
2x+3y-z=2(x+y+z)+(y-3z)=0.
\]
Thus every \(t(-4,3,1)\) is a solution, and every solution is obtained
by taking \(t=z\). Choosing \(t=1\) gives a nontrivial solution.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{example:01:homogeneous}
Let
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1&1\\2&4&-1&3\\3&6&-2&4\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The third equation of \(Ax=0\) is the sum of the first two. Subtracting
twice the first equation from the second leaves the equivalent system
\[
x_1+2x_2-x_3+x_4=0,\qquad x_3+x_4=0.
\]
Choose \(x_2=s\), \(x_4=t\). Solving backwards gives \(x_3=-t\),
\(x_1=-2s-2t\). Thus all solutions are
\[
x=\begin{pmatrix}-2s-2t\\s\\-t\\t\end{pmatrix}
=s\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
+t\begin{pmatrix}-2\\0\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad s,t\in\R.
\]
Every pair \((s,t)\) gives a solution, and every solution has this form
because its second and fourth coordinates determine \(s,t\).
The solution is nontrivial exactly when \((s,t)\ne(0,0)\).
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[More unknowns than equations]\label{thm:01:excess}
If \(A\in\R^{m\times n}\) and \(n>m\), then \(Ax=0\) has a nontrivial
solution.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We induct on the number \(m\) of equations. For one equation
\[
a_1x_1+\cdots+a_nx_n=0,\qquad n>1,
\]
if all coefficients vanish, \(x=e_1\) is a nontrivial solution.
Otherwise renumber the variables so that \(a_1\ne0\). Set
\(x_2=\cdots=x_n=1\), and then set
\[
x_1=-\frac{a_2+\cdots+a_n}{a_1}.
\]
This solves the equation and is nontrivial because \(x_2=1\).

Suppose the claim holds for \(m-1\) equations in more than \(m-1\)
unknowns. If \(A=0\), again use \(e_1\). Otherwise renumber equations
and variables so that \(a_{11}\ne0\). For each \(i=2,\ldots,m\),
subtract \(a_{i1}/a_{11}\) times the first equation from equation \(i\).
Writing \(r_i^\top\) for row \(i\), the new equations are
\[
\left(r_i-\frac{a_{i1}}{a_{11}}r_1\right)\cdot x=0,
\qquad i=2,\ldots,m.
\]
The coefficient of \(x_1\) is
\(a_{i1}-(a_{i1}/a_{11})a_{11}=0\). Hence these equations are
\[
\sum_{j=2}^n\left(a_{ij}-\frac{a_{i1}}{a_{11}}a_{1j}\right)x_j=0,
\qquad i=2,\ldots,m,
\]
a system of \(m-1\) equations in \(n-1>m-1\) unknowns.
By induction, choose a solution \((x_2,\ldots,x_n)\ne0\).
Recover the first coordinate from the first original equation:
\[
x_1=-\frac{a_{12}x_2+\cdots+a_{1n}x_n}{a_{11}}.
\]
Now \(r_1\cdot x=0\). For every remaining original row,
\[
r_i\cdot x
=\left(r_i-\frac{a_{i1}}{a_{11}}r_1\right)\cdot x
 +\frac{a_{i1}}{a_{11}}(r_1\cdot x)=0.
\]
Thus \(Ax=0\), and \(x\ne0\) because its last \(n-1\) coordinates
were chosen not all zero. This completes the induction.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{II.4. Row operations and Gauss elimination}

The \textbf{augmented matrix} \([A\mid b]\) records the coefficients and
the right-hand side of \(Ax=b\). The \textbf{elementary row operations} are
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
\text{Interchange:}&R_i\leftrightarrow R_j,&i\ne j,\\
\text{Scaling:}&R_i\leftarrow cR_i,&c\ne0,\\
\text{Replacement:}&R_i\leftarrow R_i+cR_j,&i\ne j.
\end{array}
\]
Two matrices are \textbf{row equivalent}, written \(M\sim N\), if a finite
sequence of these operations changes one into the other. The sequence
may be empty.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:01:equivalent}
Row operations on the augmented matrix preserve exactly the solutions
of a linear system.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Interchanging equations only changes their order. Multiplying an equation
by \(c\ne0\) preserves its solutions, since division by \(c\) recovers it.
For replacement, the two old equations
\[
r_i\cdot x=b_i,\qquad r_j\cdot x=b_j
\]
imply
\[
(r_i+cr_j)\cdot x=b_i+cb_j,\qquad r_j\cdot x=b_j.
\]
Conversely, subtracting \(c\) times the unchanged second equation from
the new first equation recovers \(r_i\cdot x=b_i\). Thus each operation
preserves the solution set in both directions, as does a sequence of them.
\end{proof}

The operation acts on the \emph{entire} row, including its right-hand
side. Multiplication by zero is excluded because it can discard an
equation. In a replacement, row \(j\) remains unchanged.

A matrix is in \textbf{row echelon form} if its zero rows are at the
bottom and the first nonzero entry of each successive nonzero row occurs
strictly farther to the right. These first nonzero entries are the
\textbf{pivots}; their columns are \textbf{pivot columns}.
Pivots need not equal 1. For example,
\[
\begin{pmatrix}0&2&-1&3\\0&0&0&-4\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
\]
has pivots in columns 2 and 4.

\begin{theorem}[Gauss elimination]\label{thm:01:echelon}
Every matrix is row equivalent to a matrix in row echelon form.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If the matrix is zero, it is already in echelon form. Otherwise choose
the leftmost nonzero column, say column \(j\), and interchange rows
to put a nonzero entry \(p\) in the first row. The matrix now has the form
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
0&\cdots&0&p&b_{1,j+1}&\cdots&b_{1n}\\
0&\cdots&0&b_{2j}&b_{2,j+1}&\cdots&b_{2n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&b_{mj}&b_{m,j+1}&\cdots&b_{mn}
\end{pmatrix},\qquad p\ne0.
\]
The initial \(j-1\) columns are zero. Apply
\(R_i\leftarrow R_i-(b_{ij}/p)R_1\) for \(i=2,\ldots,m\).
In column \(j\) this gives \(b_{ij}-(b_{ij}/p)p=0\); in a later
column \(k\) it gives \(b'_{ik}=b_{ik}-(b_{ij}/p)b_{1k}\). Thus
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
0&\cdots&0&p&b_{1,j+1}&\cdots&b_{1n}\\
0&\cdots&0&0&b'_{2,j+1}&\cdots&b'_{2n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&b'_{m,j+1}&\cdots&b'_{mn}
\end{pmatrix}
\]
is row equivalent to the original matrix. Repeat the procedure on the
smaller matrix
\[
B'=\begin{pmatrix}
b'_{2,j+1}&\cdots&b'_{2n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
b'_{m,j+1}&\cdots&b'_{mn}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Operations among these lower rows preserve all their earlier zero
entries. Each stage fixes a new pivot strictly to the right of the
previous pivot and leaves fewer rows and columns to process.
If the remaining matrix is zero or has no rows or columns, stop.
The fixed pivot rows followed by any zero rows form an echelon matrix.
\end{proof}

For a system, apply this procedure to the coefficient columns and carry
the right-hand side along. Write the result as \([U\mid d]\), with
\(U\) in echelon form. If \(U\) has \(r\) nonzero rows, their pivot
columns \(j_1<\cdots<j_r\) identify the \textbf{pivot variables}
\(x_{j_1},\ldots,x_{j_r}\). The other variables are \textbf{free}.

\begin{proposition}[Consistency and free variables]\label{prop:01:consistency}
The system is consistent if and only if every zero row of \(U\) has
corresponding entry zero in \(d\). If consistent, every choice of the
\(n-r\) free variables determines exactly one solution. A consistent
system has a unique solution if and only if there are no free variables.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A row \([0\ \cdots\ 0\mid d_i]\) requires \(0=d_i\). If these
requirements hold, assign arbitrary values to the free variables and
solve the nonzero rows from bottom to top. In row \(i\),
\[
x_{j_i}=\frac{d_i-\sum_{k>j_i}u_{ik}x_k}{u_{i j_i}}.
\]
The denominator is nonzero. Every variable on the right is either free
or already determined by a lower row, so the formula gives exactly one
value. This also shows that every solution is obtained. Different
choices of a free coordinate give different solutions.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{example:01:inhomogeneous}
For the matrix \(A\) of Example~\ref{example:01:homogeneous}, let
\(b=(b_1,b_2,b_3)^\top\). The operations
\[
R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1,\qquad
R_3\leftarrow R_3-3R_1,\qquad
R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_2
\]
give
\[
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr|r}
1&2&-1&1&b_1\\2&4&-1&3&b_2\\3&6&-2&4&b_3
\end{array}\right]
\sim
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr|l}
1&2&-1&1&b_1\\0&0&1&1&b_2-2b_1\\0&0&1&1&b_3-3b_1
\end{array}\right]
\]
after the first two operations, and the last operation gives
\[
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr|l}
1&2&-1&1&b_1\\0&0&1&1&b_2-2b_1\\0&0&0&0&b_3-b_1-b_2
\end{array}\right].
\]
Hence \(Ax=b\) is consistent exactly when \(b_3=b_1+b_2\).
The pivot variables are \(x_1,x_3\); the free variables are \(x_2,x_4\).
When the condition holds, back substitution gives
\[
x_2=s,\quad x_4=t,\quad x_3=b_2-2b_1-t,\qquad
x_1=b_1-2s+x_3-t=b_2-b_1-2s-2t.
\]
Thus the complete solution is
\[
x=\begin{pmatrix}b_2-b_1-2s-2t\\s\\b_2-2b_1-t\\t\end{pmatrix},
\qquad s,t\in\R.
\]
For \(b=(1,3,4)^\top\), the solutions are
\((2-2s-2t,s,1-t,t)^\top\). For \(b=(1,3,5)^\top\), there is no solution.
\end{example}

\paragraph{The same existence theorem in echelon form.}
We can now give a second proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:01:excess}.
Elimination leaves at most one pivot per row, so the number \(r\) of
pivots satisfies \(r\leqslant m<n\). Row operations leave the zero right-hand
side zero; the system is consistent. At least one variable is free.
Set one free variable to 1 and all other free variables to 0, then use
back substitution to determine the pivot variables. The resulting
solution is nonzero because its chosen free coordinate is 1.

The condition \(n>m\) is sufficient, not necessary: \(x_1+x_2=0\) and
\(2x_1+2x_2=0\) have nontrivial solutions with \(n=m=2\).
For a nonhomogeneous system, \(n>m\) does not ensure consistency, as
Example~\ref{example:01:inhomogeneous} shows.
