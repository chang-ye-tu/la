
\subsection*{1. Eigenspaces}
We first work with real vectors and real linear maps. In Section 4,
we also calculate nonreal eigenvalues and eigenvectors of real matrices.
Let \(T:V\to V\) be linear. An \emph{eigenvector} is a nonzero vector
\(v\) for which \(Tv=\lambda v\); the scalar \(\lambda\) is its
\emph{eigenvalue}. It is unique for that vector: if
\(\lambda v=\mu v\) and \(v\ne0\), then \(\lambda=\mu\).
The zero vector is not called an eigenvector. The \emph{eigenspace}
\[
 E_\lambda(T)=\ker(T-\lambda I)=\{v:Tv=\lambda v\}
\]
does include zero. Linearity gives
\(T(av+bw)=\lambda(av+bw)\) for \(v,w\in E_\lambda\); therefore
\(E_\lambda\) is a subspace, and every nonzero element of it is an
eigenvector. A linear combination of eigenvectors may be zero.

\begin{example}
For \(D=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,-1)\),
\[
 E_2(D)=\{(x,y,0)^\top:x,y\in\R\},\qquad
 E_{-1}(D)=\{(0,0,z)^\top:z\in\R\}.
\]
There are infinitely many eigenvectors, two eigenvalues, and a basis of
three eigenvectors. These are three different counts.

On the space of infinitely differentiable functions, differentiation
satisfies \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{\lambda t}=\lambda e^{\lambda t}\).
Thus \(e^{\lambda t}\) is an eigenvector of a linear map whose vectors are
functions. Infinite differentiability ensures that the derivative maps
this space into itself.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Distinct eigenvalues give independent vectors]
If \(Tv_j=\lambda_jv_j\), every \(v_j\ne0\), and
\(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k\) are distinct, then \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\)
are linearly independent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Induct on \(k\). A single nonzero vector is independent.
From \(\sum_{j=1}^k c_jv_j=0\), applying \(T-\lambda_1I\) gives
\[
 \sum_{j=2}^k c_j(\lambda_j-\lambda_1)v_j=0.
\]
The induction hypothesis and the nonzero differences give \(c_j=0\)
for \(j\geqslant2\); the original relation then gives \(c_1=0\).
\end{proof}
In particular, distinct real exponentials \(e^{\alpha_jt}\) are linearly
independent. Also the sum of eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues is
direct: in \(\sum_j w_j=0\), with \(w_j\in E_{\lambda_j}\), discard any
zero terms and apply the theorem to the remaining vectors.

\subsection*{2. The characteristic polynomial}
For \(A\in\R^{n\times n}\), define \(p_A(t)=\det(tI-A)\).
The coefficient of \(t^n\) is \(1\): only the product of diagonal entries
in the determinant expansion can supply \(n\) factors of \(t\).
Thus \(p_A\) has degree exactly \(n\).

\begin{theorem}[How eigenvalues are found]
For a scalar \(\lambda\), the following are equivalent:
\[
 \lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue of }A
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \ker(A-\lambda I)\ne\{0\}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_A(\lambda)=0.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The first equivalence is the definition. A square matrix has a nonzero
kernel precisely when it is not invertible, equivalently when its
determinant is zero. Finally
\(\det(A-\lambda I)=(-1)^n p_A(\lambda)\), so the choice of sign does
not change the roots.
\end{proof}

An eigenvalue is found from one scalar equation, but its eigenvectors
must still be found by solving the homogeneous system
\((A-\lambda I)v=0\). For a triangular \(A\),
\(p_A(t)=\prod_i(t-a_{ii})\); its diagonal entries are the eigenvalues,
with repetitions.

\begin{example}[A complete two-dimensional calculation]
Let \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\2&3\end{pmatrix}\). Then
\[
 p_A(t)=(t-1)(t-3)-8=(t-5)(t+1).
\]
For \(\lambda=5\), the equations are \(-4x+4y=0\), \(2x-2y=0\);
therefore \(E_5=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1)^\top\}\).
For \(\lambda=-1\), they reduce to \(x+2y=0\);
therefore \(E_{-1}=\operatorname{span}\{(2,-1)^\top\}\).
The two basis vectors have dot product \(1\), not zero. Distinct
eigenvalues give independence without necessarily giving orthogonality.
\end{example}

\subsection*{3. Change of basis and diagonalization}
\begin{proposition}[Similarity]
If \(B=P^{-1}AP\), then \(p_B=p_A\) and
\(E_\lambda(B)=P^{-1}E_\lambda(A)\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since \(tI-B=P^{-1}(tI-A)P\), multiplicativity gives equal determinants.
Also \(Bz=\lambda z\) if and only if \(A(Pz)=\lambda(Pz)\).
\end{proof}
Consequently the characteristic polynomial of a linear map on a
finite-dimensional space is independent of the chosen basis.

\begin{theorem}[Eigenvector basis criterion]\label{thm:08:diagonal}
A real square matrix \(A\) is \emph{diagonalizable over \(\R\)} if
and only if it has a basis of real eigenvectors. In that case
\[
 P=(v_1\ \cdots\ v_n),\qquad
 AP=P\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n),\qquad
 P^{-1}AP=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).
\]
In particular, \(n\) distinct eigenvalues give diagonalizability.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The \(j\)-th column of the middle equality is \(Av_j=\lambda_jv_j\).
If the columns form a basis, \(P\) is invertible and the last equality
follows. Conversely, a diagonalization provides those \(n\) independent
eigenvector columns. For distinct eigenvalues, their independence was
proved above.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Coordinates and powers]
For the preceding \(A\), choose
\[
 P=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 P^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Then \(AP=P\operatorname{diag}(5,-1)\). For any integer \(k\geqslant0\),
successive cancellation of \(P^{-1}P\) gives
\[
 A^k=\frac13
 \begin{pmatrix}
 5^k+2(-1)^k&2\cdot5^k-2(-1)^k\\
 5^k-(-1)^k&2\cdot5^k+(-1)^k
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
For \(k=2\), this is \(\begin{pmatrix}9&16\\8&17\end{pmatrix}\), which
direct multiplication confirms. A vector \(x\) has eigenbasis coordinates
\(P^{-1}x\); the map multiplies those coordinates by \(5\) and \(-1\).
\end{example}

The \emph{algebraic multiplicity} of \(\lambda\) is its multiplicity as
a root of \(p_A\). The \emph{geometric multiplicity} is
\(\dim E_\lambda(A)\).

\begin{proposition}[Multiplicity and missing eigenvectors]
For each eigenvalue,
\[
 1\leqslant \dim E_\lambda(A)
 \leqslant\text{algebraic multiplicity of }\lambda .
\]
If the characteristic polynomial splits into linear factors over \(\R\), \(A\) is diagonalizable exactly when equality holds for every
eigenvalue.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take a basis of \(E_\lambda\), of size \(r\), and extend it to a basis
of the whole space. In this basis the matrix has block form
\(\begin{pmatrix}\lambda I_r&B\\0&C\end{pmatrix}\).
Its characteristic polynomial is
\((t-\lambda)^r\det(tI-C)\), proving the bound.
Eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues have a direct sum. Thus their bases
together form a basis of the whole space exactly when their dimensions
sum to \(n\). When the polynomial splits, its algebraic multiplicities
sum to \(n\); the individual upper bounds make the stated equality
criterion equivalent to that dimension condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Repeated roots can leave too few eigenvectors]
For
\[
 B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&1&-1\\0&2&4\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 p_B(t)=(t-2)\{(t-1)(t-4)+2\}=(t-2)^2(t-3).
\]
At \(\lambda=2\), the first row gives \(y=0\) and the second gives
\(-y-z=0\), hence \(E_2=\operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0)^\top\}\).
At \(\lambda=3\), the equations give \(y=x\), \(z=-2y\), hence
\(E_3=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,-2)^\top\}\).
The eigenspaces together have dimension \(2<3\), so \(B\) is not
diagonalizable. By comparison, \(\operatorname{diag}(2,2,3)\) has the
same characteristic polynomial and a three-vector eigenbasis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Back substitution for a triangular matrix]
Let
\[
 C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&2\\0&5&-1\\0&0&7\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its eigenvalues are \(1,5,7\). Back substitution gives, respectively,
\[
 v_1=(1,0,0)^\top,\quad
 v_5=(1/4,1,0)^\top,\quad
 v_7=(1/4,-1/2,1)^\top.
\]
For \(\lambda=7\), the second equation is \(-2y-z=0\);
the first is \(-6x+y+2z=0\). Setting \(z=1\) gives the stated vector.
Thus
\[
 P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/4&1/4\\0&1&-1/2\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
 \quad
 P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1/4&-3/8\\0&1&1/2\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
\]
satisfy \(P^{-1}CP=\operatorname{diag}(1,5,7)\).
Here every calculation uses triangular substitution; no general formula
for a cubic root is needed.
\end{example}


\subsection*{4. Real matrices can have nonreal eigenvalues}
A complex number has the form \(a+bi\), where \(a,b\in\R\) and
\(i^2=-1\). Addition is componentwise, and multiplication gives
\[
 (a+bi)(c+di)=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.
\]
Its conjugate is \(\overline{a+bi}=a-bi\), and its absolute value is
\(|a+bi|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). Conjugation preserves sums and products.
For \(a+bi\ne0\), multiplication by its conjugate gives
\[
 \frac1{a+bi}=\frac{a-bi}{a^2+b^2}.
\]
These arithmetic rules suffice for the calculations below.

For a monic real quadratic \(t^2-bt+c\), completing the square gives
\[
 t^2-bt+c=\left(t-\frac b2\right)^2-\frac{b^2-4c}{4}.
\]
If \(\Delta=b^2-4c\geqslant0\), its roots are
\((b\pm\sqrt\Delta)/2\). If \(\Delta<0\), use
\((i\sqrt{-\Delta})^2=\Delta\) to obtain
\[
 \lambda_\pm=\frac{b\pm i\sqrt{-\Delta}}2.
\]
They are nonreal conjugates. Thus a negative discriminant means
no \emph{real} root, not absence of eigenvalues.

For real \(A\), a complex column \(z=x+iy\) consists of two real
columns \(x,y\). Define \(Az=Ax+iAy\). Writing \(\lambda=a+bi\),
the equation \(Az=\lambda z\) is equivalent to the two real equations
\[
 Ax=ax-by,\qquad Ay=bx+ay.                         \tag{*}
\]
A nonzero complex column satisfying them is an eigenvector for \(\lambda\).
The criterion \(p_A(\lambda)=0\) still finds these eigenvalues:
elimination and the determinant criterion use only addition,
multiplication and division by nonzero numbers, which the arithmetic
above permits. The resulting eigenvector must satisfy every row of
\((A-\lambda I)z=0\).

\paragraph{Conjugate pairs.}
If \(Az=\lambda z\), then the real entries of \(A\) give
\[
 A\bar z=\overline{Az}=\overline{\lambda z}
       =\bar\lambda\,\bar z,\qquad \bar z\ne0.
\]
Equivalently, for the real coefficients \(c_j\) of \(p_A\),
\[
 p_A(\bar\lambda)=\sum_jc_j\bar\lambda^j
                =\overline{\sum_jc_j\lambda^j}
                =\overline{p_A(\lambda)}=0.
\]
Conjugating a factorization
\(p_A(t)=(t-\lambda)^r q(t)\), \(q(\lambda)\ne0\), shows that
\(\bar\lambda\) has the same multiplicity.
When \(\lambda\) is real, (*) gives \(Ax=\lambda x\) and
\(Ay=\lambda y\); at least one is a nonzero real eigenvector.
When \(\lambda\) is nonreal, no nonzero \emph{real} eigenvector can
have that eigenvalue: the imaginary part of \(Av=\lambda v\)
would give \(0=bv\).

A real eigenbasis must consist of real vectors. In particular, a real
diagonalization requires \(n\) independent real eigenvectors.
We say that a degree-\(n\) polynomial \emph{splits over \(\R\)} if it
is a product of \(n\) real linear factors, with repetitions allowed.
A real characteristic polynomial need not have this property.

\begin{proposition}[Coefficients and eigenvalues]
For a real \(n\times n\) matrix, the coefficient of \(t^{n-1}\) in
\(p_A(t)\) is \(-\tr A\), and \(p_A(0)=(-1)^n\det A\).
If a factorization \(p_A(t)=\prod_{j=1}^n(t-\lambda_j)\) is given,
allowing real or nonreal \(\lambda_j\), then
\[
 \det A=\prod_{j=1}^n\lambda_j,\qquad
 \tr A=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j.
\]
Every real matrix of odd order has at least one real eigenvalue.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In the permutation expansion of \(\det(tI-A)\), the identity permutation
contributes \(\prod_i(t-a_{ii})\), whose \(t^{n-1}\) coefficient is
\(-\sum_i a_{ii}\). Every other permutation moves at least two indices,
so its term has degree at most \(n-2\). Evaluation at zero gives the
constant coefficient. Comparing these two coefficients with the stated
factorization proves the sum and product formulas.
For odd \(n\), the monic polynomial \(p_A(t)\) is positive for sufficiently
large positive \(t\) and negative for sufficiently large negative \(t\).
The intermediate value theorem supplies a real root, hence an eigenvalue.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The eigenvalues of a rotation]
For
\[
 R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad c=\cos\theta,\quad s=\sin\theta,
\]
\[
 p_R(t)=(t-c)^2+s^2,\qquad \lambda_\pm=c\pm is.
\]
If \(s\ne0\), these are nonreal and their eigenvectors necessarily
have nonreal coordinates. Direct multiplication gives
\[
 R(\theta)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}c+is\\s-ic\end{pmatrix}
 =(c+is)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Conjugating gives the eigenvector \((1,i)^\top\) for \(c-is\).
There is no real eigenvector, and therefore no real eigenbasis.
If \(s=0\), then \(R=\pm I\), and every nonzero real vector is an
eigenvector with eigenvalue \(c=\pm1\).
For a quarter turn, \(c=0,s=1\), the eigenvalues are \(i,-i\):
they are purely imaginary, meaning their real parts vanish.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Solving a nonreal eigenvalue equation]
For the real matrix \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\3&1\end{pmatrix}\),
\[
 p_A(t)=(t-2)(t-1)+3=t^2-3t+5,\qquad
 \lambda_\pm=\frac{3\pm i\sqrt{11}}2.
\]
The homogeneous equations are
\[
 (2-\lambda)x-y=0,\qquad 3x+(1-\lambda)y=0.
\]
The first gives \(y=(2-\lambda)x\). Set \(x=1\), giving
\(z_\lambda=(1,2-\lambda)^\top\). In the second equation,
\[
 3+(1-\lambda)(2-\lambda)=\lambda^2-3\lambda+5=0,
\]
so both rows hold. If \(x=0\), the first row forces \(y=0\);
hence all eigenvectors for this root are precisely the nonzero complex
multiples of \(z_\lambda\).
The two displayed eigenvectors are conjugates. There are no real
eigenvalues and no real eigenbasis.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Reading: eigenvalues of real orthogonal matrices.}
For \(A^\top A=I\), a nonreal eigenvalue still has a conjugate partner
because \(A\) is real. To find its absolute value, use (*) and real norms:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \norm x^2+\norm y^2
 &=\norm{Ax}^2+\norm{Ay}^2\\
 &=\norm{ax-by}^2+\norm{bx+ay}^2\\
 &=(a^2+b^2)(\norm x^2+\norm y^2).
 \end{aligned}
\]
The cross terms cancel. Since \(z=x+iy\ne0\), the final common factor
is positive, and \(a^2+b^2=1\). Thus \(|\lambda|=1\).
A real eigenvalue is consequently \(1\) or \(-1\), but the rotation
shows why these need not be all the eigenvalues.

\subsection*{5. Diagonal differential equations}
If \(F'(t)=DF(t)\), \(D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)\),
then each coordinate solves \(f_j'=\lambda_j f_j\).
The derivative of \(e^{-\lambda_jt}f_j(t)\) is zero, so
\(f_j(t)=c_je^{\lambda_jt}\), including when \(\lambda_j=0\).
The functions \(e^{\lambda_jt}e_j\) form a basis of the solution space:
they span by the formula, and evaluation at \(t=0\) proves independence.
If \(A=PDP^{-1}\), putting \(G=P^{-1}F\) gives \(G'=DG\), hence
\[
 F(t)=P\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1t},\ldots,e^{\lambda_nt})P^{-1}F(0).
\]
For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\2&3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(F(0)=(1,0)^\top\),
\(P^{-1}F(0)=(1/3,1/3)^\top\); consequently
\[
 F(t)=\frac13\{e^{5t}(1,1)^\top+e^{-t}(2,-1)^\top\}.
\]
Differentiation verifies \(F'=AF\), and at zero it gives \((1,0)^\top\).
This is the same change of coordinates used for powers of \(A\).

\subsection*{Reading: eigenvectors when cubic roots are implicit}
For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&2\\-2&1&3\\1&-1&1\end{pmatrix}\), set
\(u=t-1\). Then \(p_A(t)=u^3-u-1\). Neither \(1\) nor \(-1\)
is a root, so the rational-root test rules out rational roots.
Its derivative is \(3u^2-1\). At both stationary points, in particular
at the local maximum \(-1/\sqrt3\), the polynomial is negative:
the latter value is \(2/(3\sqrt3)-1<0\).
The function therefore has exactly one real root. That root is simple,
because neither stationary point is a root.

For that real root \(u\), solve the first two equations with \(z=1\):
\[
 ux+y=2,\qquad 2x+uy=3,
 \quad
 x=\frac{2u-3}{u^2-2},\qquad y=\frac{3u-4}{u^2-2}.
\]
The denominator cannot vanish at a root: \(u^2=2\) would give
\(u^3-u-1=u-1\ne0\).
The third equation is \(x-y-u=0\); substitution makes its numerator
\(1+u-u^3=0\). Thus these formulas give a basis vector for the unique
real eigenspace. Its dimension is one, so this real matrix has no real
eigenbasis. One eigenspace contains infinitely many eigenvectors.

\subsection*{Reading: triangularization with a real-root hypothesis}
Suppose \(p_A\) splits over \(\R\). Then a real change of basis makes
\(A\) upper triangular. To prove this, induct on the order \(n\).
The assertion is immediate for \(n=1\). Choose a real root \(\lambda_1\)
and a nonzero vector \(v_1\in\ker(A-\lambda_1I)\).
Extend \(v_1\) to a real basis, with column matrix \(P\). Then
\[
 P^{-1}AP=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&b\\0&B\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad p_A(t)=(t-\lambda_1)p_B(t).
\]
Polynomial cancellation shows that \(p_B\) also splits into real factors.
By induction there is a real invertible \(S\) with \(S^{-1}BS\)
upper triangular. The change of basis \(P\operatorname{diag}(1,S)\)
gives
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&bS\\0&S^{-1}BS\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its diagonal entries are the real roots of \(p_A\), with multiplicity.
The change of basis is not asserted to be orthogonal.
The root hypothesis is essential: the rotation above cannot be similar
over \(\R\) to a triangular matrix, whose diagonal entries would be
real eigenvalues.

\section*{Exercises and solutions}
A star marks an advanced exercise. Matrices are real. The quadratic,
conjugate-pair and skew-symmetric tasks include nonreal eigenvalues and
use complex columns when needed; other vector-space and diagonalization
tasks retain their stated real scope. Root multiplicities refer to factors
of the characteristic polynomial. A complete list of \(n\) real eigenvalues
is used only when a real factorization has been proved or explicitly assumed.
