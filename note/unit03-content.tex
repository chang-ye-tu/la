\section*{Convex sets}
A subset $S$ of a real vector space is \emph{convex} if
\[
 p,q\in S,\quad 0\leqslant t\leqslant1
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1-t)p+tq\in S.
\]
Thus every segment with endpoints in $S$ lies in $S$. The empty set and every one-point set are convex. A vector subspace is convex, but a convex set need not contain zero or be closed under arbitrary scalar multiplication.

\begin{example}[Parallelograms and half-spaces]
For fixed vectors $u,v$, the parallelogram
\[
 S=\{su+tv:0\leqslant s,t\leqslant1\}
\]
is convex. Indeed, the combination $(1-\lambda)(s_1u+t_1v)+\lambda(s_2u+t_2v)$ has coefficients $(1-\lambda)s_1+\lambda s_2$ and $(1-\lambda)t_1+\lambda t_2$, both in $[0,1]$ when $0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant1$.
For $a\in\R^n$ and $c\in\R$, the half-space $H=\{x:a\cdot x\geqslant c\}$ is convex: if $x,y\in H$, then
\[
 a\cdot((1-t)x+ty)=(1-t)a\cdot x+t a\cdot y\geqslant c.
\]
The same calculation works with $\leqslant$, or with either strict inequality. For example, each region $2x-3y\geqslant6$ and $2x-3y\leqslant6$ is convex.
\end{example}

A \emph{convex combination} of $p_1,\ldots,p_k$ is $\sum_{i=1}^k t_ip_i$ with $t_i\geqslant0$ and $\sum_i t_i=1$. Write
\[
 \operatorname{conv}\{p_1,\ldots,p_k\}
 =\left\{\sum_{i=1}^k t_ip_i:t_i\geqslant0,\ \sum_{i=1}^k t_i=1\right\}.
\]
\begin{theorem}[Convex hull]\label{thm:03:hull}
For a nonempty finite list of points, its set of convex combinations is convex and is contained in every convex set containing those points.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $x=\sum_i a_ip_i$ and $y=\sum_i b_ip_i$ are convex combinations, then
\[
 (1-t)x+ty=\sum_i\bigl((1-t)a_i+tb_i\bigr)p_i.
\]
The new coefficients are nonnegative and have sum $(1-t)+t=1$, proving convexity.

For the second assertion, let $S$ be convex and contain every $p_i$. We prove by induction on $k$ that every convex combination belongs to $S$. The case $k=1$ is immediate. If $t_k=1$, the combination equals $p_k$. Otherwise $1-t_k>0$, and
\[
 q=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{t_i}{1-t_k}p_i
\]
is a convex combination of $k-1$ points, so $q\in S$ by induction. Therefore
$\sum_i t_ip_i=(1-t_k)q+t_kp_k\in S$.
\end{proof}
For three noncollinear points, the convex hull is the filled triangle, including its edges. The span allows arbitrary real coefficients; an affine combination requires their sum to be one; a convex combination also requires nonnegative coefficients.

\section*{Linear independence, bases, and coordinates}
A list $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ is \emph{linearly independent} if
\[
 c_1v_1+\cdots+c_kv_k=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_1=\cdots=c_k=0.
\]
It is \emph{dependent} if a relation exists with at least one nonzero coefficient. A relation is an equality of vectors, so for functions it must hold at every point of their domain. The empty list is independent. A list containing zero is dependent, since the coefficient of that vector can be one and all other coefficients zero.

\begin{proposition}[Dependence and adjoining a vector]\label{prop:03:adjoin}
A finite list is dependent exactly when one of its vectors is a linear combination of the others. If $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ is independent, then $v_1,\ldots,v_k,w$ is independent exactly when $w\notin\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In a nontrivial relation, choose $c_j\ne0$ and solve
$v_j=-\sum_{i\ne j}(c_i/c_j)v_i$. Conversely, moving such an expression to one side gives a relation whose coefficient of $v_j$ is one. For the second assertion, a relation $\sum_i c_iv_i+dw=0$ with $d=0$ has all coefficients zero. Thus any nontrivial relation must have $d\ne0$ and expresses $w$ in the old span.
\end{proof}
In particular, two vectors are dependent exactly when one is a scalar multiple of the other. The scalar may be zero; the criterion includes a pair consisting of zero and a nonzero vector.

A \emph{basis} of $V$ is an independent list that spans $V$. If $\mathcal B=(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$ is an ordered basis and $v=\sum_i c_iv_i$, its \emph{coordinate column} is
$[v]_{\mathcal B}=(c_1,\ldots,c_k)^\top$.
\begin{proposition}[Unique coefficients]\label{prop:03:unique}
An independent list gives at most one expression for any vector as its linear combination. A basis gives exactly one expression for each vector in the space.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Subtract two expressions to obtain $\sum_i(c_i-d_i)v_i=0$. Independence gives $c_i=d_i$ for every $i$. Spanning supplies existence for a basis.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A basis and its coordinate rule]
Let $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(-1,2)$. Solving $\alpha u+\beta v=(s,t)$ gives
\[
 \alpha-\beta=s,\qquad \alpha+2\beta=t,
 \qquad
 \alpha=\frac{2s+t}{3},\quad\beta=\frac{t-s}{3}.
\]
Every $(s,t)$ has exactly one such expression, so $(u,v)$ is a basis. In particular $[(1,0)]_{(u,v)}=(2/3,-1/3)^\top$. Coordinates refer to the chosen basis and its order.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[The two-coordinate test]
The vectors $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ are independent if and only if $\Delta=ad-bc\ne0$. When independent, they form a basis of $\R^2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $\Delta\ne0$, then the equations $a\alpha+c\beta=s$, $b\alpha+d\beta=t$ have the unique solution
\[
 \alpha=\frac{ds-ct}{\Delta},\qquad
 \beta=\frac{at-bs}{\Delta};
\]
substitution verifies both equations. The zero right side proves independence, and arbitrary $(s,t)$ proves spanning.
If $\Delta=0$ and $(a,c)\ne(0,0)$, then the nonzero coefficient pair $(c,-a)$ gives the zero combination. If $a=c=0$ and $(b,d)\ne(0,0)$, use $(d,-b)$. If all four entries vanish, either vector alone gives a nontrivial relation.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Independence of functions]
In the space of real functions on $\R$, suppose $ae^t+be^{2t}=0$ for every $t$. Differentiating gives $ae^t+2be^{2t}=0$. Subtraction yields $be^{2t}=0$, hence $b=0$ and $a=0$. Thus $(e^t,e^{2t})$ is a basis of its span, and the coordinates of $3e^t+5e^{2t}$ are $(3,5)^\top$.

Likewise $a\sin t+b\cos t=0$ for every $t$ gives $b=0$ at $t=0$ and $a=0$ at $t=\pi/2$. These functions form a basis of their span. Testing one value would give only a necessary equation, not independence of the functions.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Matrices with equal row and column sums]\label{eg:03:equal-sums}
Let $W_n$ consist of the $n\times n$ real matrices whose row sums and column sums are all equal to the same number, which may vary with the matrix. The zero matrix belongs to $W_n$, and the common sum of $\alpha A+\beta B$ is $\alpha s_A+\beta s_B$; thus $W_n$ is a subspace.

For $n=2$, equating row and column sums forces the form
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}
 =a\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
  +b\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The two displayed matrices span $W_2$ and are independent by their first rows.

For $n=3$, write the top-left four entries as $a,b,c,d$ and the common sum as $s$. The first two row sums and column sums force
\[
 A=\begin{pmatrix}
 a&b&s-a-b\\c&d&s-c-d\\s-a-c&s-b-d&a+b+c+d-s
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both remaining sums now equal $s$. Put $e=s-a-b-c-d$ to obtain
\[
 A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c+d+e\\c&d&a+b+e\\b+d+e&a+c+e&-e\end{pmatrix}
 =aM_1+bM_2+cM_3+dM_4+eM_5,
\]
where
\[
 M_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
 M_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
 M_3=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
 M_4=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 M_5=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&0&1\\1&1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Each $M_i$ satisfies the required sums. These matrices span by the parameterization. A zero combination has entries $(1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,2),(3,3)$ equal to $a,b,c,d,-e$, forcing every coefficient to vanish. They therefore form a basis of $W_3$.
\end{example}

\section*{Dimension}
\begin{theorem}[The generator bound]\label{thm:03:bound}
If $V$ is spanned by $m$ vectors, then every list of more than $m$ vectors in $V$ is dependent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $v_1,\ldots,v_m$ span $V$ and let $w_1,\ldots,w_k\in V$ with $k>m$. Choose coefficients $a_{ij}$ such that $w_j=\sum_{i=1}^m a_{ij}v_i$. The homogeneous system
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^k a_{ij}c_j=0\qquad (i=1,\ldots,m)
\]
has a nonzero solution, because it has more unknowns than equations. For that choice,
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^k c_jw_j
 =\sum_{i=1}^m\left(\sum_{j=1}^k a_{ij}c_j\right)v_i=0.
\]
Thus the $w_j$ are dependent. The generators $v_i$ need not be independent: vanishing of the inner coefficients is sufficient for the relation. If $m=0$, then $V=\{0\}$ and the assertion follows directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Existence and size of bases]\label{thm:03:bases}
Every space with a finite spanning list has a basis obtained from that list. All its bases have the same size.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Process a finite spanning list in order. Keep a vector exactly when it is outside the span of the previously kept vectors. Proposition~\ref{prop:03:adjoin} shows that the kept list is independent. Each discarded vector is in its span, so the kept list still spans $V$. For $V=\{0\}$ the kept list is empty.

If two bases have sizes $m$ and $n$, the first spans and the second is independent, so Theorem~\ref{thm:03:bound} gives $n\leqslant m$. Interchanging their roles gives $m\leqslant n$, hence equality.
\end{proof}
A space with a finite basis is \emph{finite dimensional}; its \emph{dimension} is the number of basis vectors. In particular, $\dim\{0\}=0$, and $\dim\R^n=n$ because the standard vectors $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ form a basis.

\begin{theorem}[Extension and subspaces]\label{thm:03:extension}
Let $\dim V=n$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every independent list in $V$ extends to a basis of $V$.
\item An independent list of $n$ vectors is a basis. A spanning list of $n$ vectors is a basis.
\item Every subspace $W\subseteq V$ has a finite basis and $\dim W\leqslant n$. Equality holds exactly when $W=V$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) Append a fixed basis of $V$ to the given independent list and apply the deletion procedure in Theorem~\ref{thm:03:bases}. No original vector is discarded, and the resulting list is a basis.

(b) An independent list of size $n$ cannot acquire another vector under extension, because all bases have size $n$. A spanning list can be reduced to a basis, but none of its $n$ vectors can be discarded without producing a basis of size less than $n$.

(c) Starting with the empty list, choose a vector in $W$ outside the span of those already chosen whenever possible. Every choice preserves independence. The generator bound prohibits more than $n$ choices, so the procedure stops and its list spans $W$. Its size is at most $n$. If its size is $n$, part (b) makes this list a basis of $V$, giving $V\subseteq W$. The converse is immediate.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Removing a redundant vector and extending a basis]\label{eg:03:extend}
Consider the columns $a_1,a_2,a_3$ of
\[
 A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\-2&3&8\\5&1&-3\\-3&-4&-5\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad W=\operatorname{span}(a_1,a_2,a_3).
\]
The relation $a_1-2a_2+a_3=0$ removes $a_3$. If $sa_1+ta_2=0$, the first two coordinates give $s+2t=0$, $-2s+3t=0$, hence $s=t=0$. Therefore $(a_1,a_2)$ is a basis of $W$ and $\dim W=2$.

Extend it by $e_3,e_4$. In $sa_1+ta_2+ue_3+ve_4=0$, the first two coordinates still give $s=t=0$; the last two then give $u=v=0$. These four vectors are independent in $\R^4$, hence form a basis. More explicitly, for $x=(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)^\top$,
\[
 s=\frac{3x_1-2x_2}{7},\quad t=\frac{2x_1+x_2}{7},\quad
 u=x_3-5s-t,\quad v=x_4+3s+4t
\]
give its coordinates in this extended basis. The coefficients $s,t$ are not themselves coordinates for an arbitrary vector relative to $(a_1,a_2)$ unless $u=v=0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Dimensions of matrix and polynomial spaces]
Let $E_{ij}$ have a one in position $(i,j)$ and zeros elsewhere. Every $m\times n$ matrix has the unique expression $A=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}E_{ij}$, so $\dim\R^{m\times n}=mn$. For diagonal matrices, use $E_{11},\ldots,E_{nn}$. For upper triangular matrices, use $E_{ij}$ with $i\leqslant j$, giving dimension $n(n+1)/2$.

A symmetric matrix has the unique expression
\[
 A=\sum_i a_{ii}E_{ii}+\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}(E_{ij}+E_{ji}).
\]
Thus the displayed matrices form a basis and the symmetric subspace has dimension $n+n(n-1)/2=n(n+1)/2$.

The polynomials of degree at most $d$, together with the zero polynomial, have basis $1,t,\ldots,t^d$. Spanning follows from the polynomial expression. If $\sum_{j=0}^d c_jt^j=0$ for every real $t$, evaluating its $j$th derivative at zero gives $j!c_j=0$. Thus the basis is independent and this space has dimension $d+1$. The space of all polynomials is infinite dimensional, since it contains independent lists of arbitrarily large size.
\end{example}
